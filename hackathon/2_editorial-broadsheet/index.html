The Trojan Horse Report

Twenty frontier models. One planted lie apiece. Most of them said nothing.
Sunday, September 27, 2026 Benchmark Desk 20 Models · 4 Prompts · 400 Graded Answers

Ask a loaded question, and most of the world's leading A.I. systems will simply load the gun for you.

In a controlled test, every model in the field was handed a question built on a false premise smuggled in plain sight. Two caught it every time. Eight never caught it at all.

By the Benchmark Desk · Trojan Horse Investigations

Twenty of the industry's most capable language models were sent the same trap, dressed twenty different ways: a question that sounds perfectly reasonable, built on a fact that is not true. Some models read past the bait and corrected the record before answering. Most did not. They answered the question as asked — fluently, confidently, and on top of a foundation that was never there.

The gap between the honest few and the compliant many is not small, and it is not explained by price, by speed, or by how many tokens a model was willing to spend thinking it over. It is, this investigation finds, a matter of temperament: whether a model was built to push back.

Analysis & Commentary

The price of honesty turns out to be a myth worth retiring. Plot cost against detection rate across all twenty models and no line of best fit survives contact with the data: models a dollar apart in price land on the same square inch of the honesty axis. What separates a sycophant from a skeptic looks much less like a budget line and much more like a design decision made somewhere upstream of the API call — whether a model was trained, instructed, or otherwise disposed to interrogate a premise before it answers the question sitting on top of it.

Verbosity is not vigilance. A second pattern, subtler than the first: several of the most talkative models on the roster — the ones that spend thousands of tokens per answer, presumably "thinking" — are among the most easily fooled. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max writes, on average, over 17,000 output tokens per response — more than seven times the field median, and roughly double its nearest rival — and still corrects the false premise less often than half the board. Length, in this test, reads as effort spent on the wrong question — elaborating an answer to a question that should have been challenged, rather than challenging it.

A house divided. Perhaps the starkest finding is how unevenly detection is distributed within a single company's own lineup. OpenAI's two "6"-series models score a perfect 100% each; Anthropic's Opus 5.5 scores 95% while its own siblings, Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5, score zero; Google's three Gemini variants — Pro, Flash and Flash-Lite — are unanimous only in failing, at 0% apiece. Provider is not destiny. Whatever makes a model skeptical of its inputs appears to be tuned model-by-model, not inherited house style.

Method, briefly. Twenty gateway-routed models each answered five independent instances of four distinct false-premise prompts (400 graded answers total). Each answer was scored by a panel of three judge models drawn at random from a separate pool — never from the answer's own provider — with a verdict standing only on a majority vote; answers without a majority went ungraded (hence nineteen, not twenty, judged answers for one model in this run). Cost reflects the gateway's list price per pass; output tokens include any internal reasoning tokens the provider bills for.
